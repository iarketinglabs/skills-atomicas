# Evidence, claims, and process governance

Use this reference when a process depends on standards, regulation, internal policy, safety controls, named roles, or claims about how work is currently performed.

## Track evidence status

Label material as one of the following when the distinction affects a decision:

- **Confirmed source:** supported by a supplied, identifiable, applicable document or current primary source.
- **Owner-confirmed:** explicitly confirmed by a responsible role; record who/role and when where appropriate.
- **Observed sample:** seen in a bounded sample of work, logs, or direct observation. Record the sample and date; it does not prove all cases behave the same way.
- **Inference:** a reasoned interpretation of available material. State the basis and uncertainty.
- **Proposal:** a designed future state or suggested control, not an existing practice.
- **Unknown:** evidence is absent, contradictory, stale, or inaccessible.

Do not turn an inference into policy or a proposal into a current-state fact. For conflicts, show each source, date, owner, and the decision that remains open.

## Source quality and current requirements

For claims about law, standards, safety, or regulated activity:

1. Identify jurisdiction, sector, activity, and applicable date.
2. Prefer the issuing authority, official standard body, regulator, or primary technical specification. Use secondary summaries only for orientation.
3. Verify the current edition, effective date, transition period, scope, and exact clause before stating a requirement.
4. Record title, issuer, edition or publication date, relevant section, and access date when the deliverable needs traceability.
5. If the source cannot be checked, state that verification is pending and name the role that should review it.

When inheriting a research synthesis, inspect the evidence for each decision-relevant claim rather than relying on the report’s overall source count or status labels. Separate primary standards and regulator materials from certification-body interpretation, vendor documentation, consultant commentary, and single-source estimates. Vendor documentation can establish what that product documents; it does not by itself establish independent effectiveness. Treat editions, transition dates, draft initiatives, benchmark results, and regulatory amendments as time-sensitive until checked against the official source.

The source synthesis used to shape this skill did not include its complete evidence register and marked several popular process heuristics as unverified. Accordingly, do not treat a fixed “five-minute rule,” a prescribed page or step count, a mandatory site visit, a fixed checklist size, or a purported standard clause as authoritative without a current, identifiable source and local approval.

## Governance responsibilities

Assign roles, not only people:

- **Process owner:** accountable for intended outcome, boundaries, measures, and process changes.
- **Procedure owner/editor:** maintains the document and change history; this may be the same role as process owner.
- **Approver/control owner:** confirms delegated authority, risk, quality, legal, security, or safety controls where applicable.
- **Executor/user:** performs the work and reports ambiguity or operational change.
- **System owner:** verifies permissions, interfaces, logging, and technical dependencies for automation.

Use a responsibility matrix only when role clarity is a real need. If used, define one accountable role for each decision or deliverable and show consulted/informed roles only where they change execution. A matrix does not replace an approval or delegation policy.

## Version, access, and change

Maintain one identifiable current version, approval status, effective date, source references, review owner, and change history. Trigger review when a relevant policy, system, risk, role, interface, regulation, or outcome measure changes. Prevent obsolete copies from appearing current; preserve retired versions only according to records policy.

## Limitations

An SOP is not evidence that people follow it, that a control works, that a process is compliant, or that an agent is safe. Those conclusions require appropriate records, testing, observation, authority, and scope. State what was actually checked and what remains outside the review.
