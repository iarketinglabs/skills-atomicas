# Production and Retention Guidance

Use this reference when the request concerns video concepts, scripts, titles, thumbnails, Shorts, or weak retention. Adapt the guidance to the audience, format, accessibility needs, and available evidence.

## Packaging: title and thumbnail

Treat title and thumbnail as a single proposition:

- Make the topic, benefit, tension, or outcome legible to the intended viewer.
- Use a gap in knowledge only when the video resolves it promptly and honestly.
- Give the thumbnail one dominant idea. Remove text or objects that make it ambiguous at small size.
- Generate at least two directions when the stakes justify it: a tension-led angle and an outcome-led angle.
- Ensure the opening confirms the premise before expanding context.

## Opening diagnostic: proof, value, structure, stakes

In the opening, answer as many of these as the format needs:

| Element | Question |
|---|---|
| Proof | Why should the viewer trust that this is worth watching? |
| Value | What useful, interesting, or emotionally meaningful outcome will they receive? |
| Structure | What path will the video take so the viewer can orient themselves? |
| Stakes | Why does the answer, decision, or story matter now? |

Avoid formulaic recitation. A visual result may supply proof; a question may establish stakes; a short outline can give structure.

## Progression and cognitive load

Retention improves when the viewer experiences clear progress. Use a sequence such as problem, attempt, evidence, complication, resolution, or claim, demonstration, interpretation, next action.

- Add a meaningful takeaway before attention naturally drops, but do not manufacture interruptions without informational purpose.
- Change scenes, camera angles, graphics, demonstrations, or examples when they clarify a new beat.
- Keep visual and audio signals aligned with the main point. Fast edits, dense captions, and overlapping graphics can reduce comprehension.
- Use stories only when they illuminate the decision or insight. Avoid anecdotes that delay the promised answer.
- Use chapters, headings, summaries, or recurring visual language when they help long-form orientation.

## Long-form and Shorts

Choose the format from the job to be done.

- Use long-form when the viewer benefits from nuance, process, proof, comparison, personality, or a complete narrative arc.
- Use Shorts when one idea can create a self-contained payoff quickly: an observation, demonstration, contradiction, answer, or invitation to a deeper piece.
- Do not turn an arbitrary clip into a Short. Recut it so the premise, payoff, and context work independently.
- Link formats through shared themes, series, and next-viewing paths instead of assuming that short-form viewers will become long-form viewers.

## Script and edit checklist

Before publishing, review:

1. Does the opening fulfill the title and thumbnail immediately enough for the video's context?
2. Can the viewer name the main promise after the first section?
3. Is every beat either advancing the argument/story or building necessary understanding?
4. Does each proof point have a clear source, demonstration, or caveat?
5. Are captions, visual contrasts, and audio understandable on common viewing conditions?
6. Does the close lead to a relevant next choice rather than a generic demand for engagement?
